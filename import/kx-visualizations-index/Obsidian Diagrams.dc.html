KITDiagram kit index — every variable needed to draw a new one
Mermaid buys speed and costs control: fixed node shapes, right-angle routing, no say over where a label sits. These are authored as SVG paths instead. Colour now lives in a hard backdrop — a copy of the shape offset four pixels right and down, zero blur, full opacity — so a node reads as a lifted card rather than an outlined box. Six node types, three frame types, six connectors, five terminators. Nothing is generated at runtime.

Beside, it takes a 12–32px hairline leader at rgba(255,255,255,.22) from the bar’s mid-height; a label set deep in a gutter takes the same hairline run vertically from the bar’s edge instead. In 4c that lands 19 above or below and 5 beside, with 7 leaders — 5 horizontal, 2 vertical. Attribution without a leader is horizontal, never a matter of distance: the label must sit over the bar it names — all 17 of them overlap their own bar by at least 16px, and never less than 61.6% of that bar’s width — with no other bar of that row beneath the words, and 7.97px of painted air to the bar, halo included. Distance alone cannot carry it: an above-or-below label sits about 10px of ink off its own bar while a neighbour in the same row is only 2–8px further, so a distance margin is unreachable by construction, and anything that cannot sit over its bar takes a leader instead — which is also what licenses a placement deep in a tall gutter, where proximity would attribute the label to the wrong row. Reserve every connector’s departure and arrival stub before placing, since the space directly above a bar is the same space its handoff needs. Where both compete, the label may intrude on a stub reservation but never on drawn geometry — the reservation is a heuristic, the bar is a fact handoff · shared boundaryone continuous curve, not a cornered route, and a single cubic wherever the route allows: 10 of 4c’s 14 are one cubic, 4 take one waypoint and emit two. It leaves and lands 8px clear of the middle of a bar edge — all 28 endpoints do, exactly — and the tangent at each end is that edge’s normal, so the exit and the entry are square to the bar with no straight stub standing in front of them. The handle length is what makes it read as fluid rather than plumbed: half the travel measured along that same normal, floored at 10px and capped at 92, so a long climb bows across its whole length while a short sideways step turns tight instead of ballooning past its target. Curvature is spread over the travel and never spent in a corner, and no segment’s tangent turns back on its own chord at any point — that reversal is what shows up as a kink at the tail or a small loop under the arrowhead, and it is the thing to test for, since it survives every clearance check. The midpoint has to outrank path length in the scoring, not merely be offered as one option among mid ±6/12/18: score on length alone and anchors wander to a corner to save two or three px of arc, which is the “random corner” read. Where an edge is already claimed the second handoff moves to a different edge and takes that midpoint; where the geometry leaves no other edge, the two sit symmetric about the midpoint of a wide one — 1004 in and 1036 out on the 64px bar at 988@330 — never off-centre alone. Waypoints sit on gutter midlines and are chosen for clearance, not for shape, and a route is only allowed one if the direct cubic fails: try direct first, always, or the drawing fills with corridors it did not need. Top and bottom edges are the default; a side edge at mid-height is legal where a bar carries no beside-label, and 9 of the 14 connectors use one (11 such anchors in all). Anchors come from a shared registry, 12px apart at the least, or two arrowheads land on the same point and the pair reads as one line passing through the bar — the closest pair in the drawing is 24.8px. The registry and the route search run together: allocate as you route, narrowest bars first, since a 16px bar has a 4px usable span and cannot hold two anchors on one edge at all. Clearance is measured on the sampled curve at 0.5px against rendered ink, and the floors it holds are 8px to every bar, 10.1px to the nearest label, 22.5px to the nearest leader, and the lane column left of x=112 untouched sealed corridortwo of 4c’s sixteen handoffs are not drawn: 780@330 → 812@442 and 956@554 → 972@442. Routes exist for both — 6.66× and 4.55× their direct distance — but a curve that crosses half the panel to move a step sideways is the defect, not the fix, so they are omitted rather than drawn. Both were retested with the blocking label shifted through every position that still clears its bar, and neither yields anything under 2.6×. The cause is arithmetic: a bar narrower than its label plus two 20px stubs has no side left for both, and bar 812@442 is 48px wide with a 100px label and anchors on both edges. Four bars are in that condition at 24 steps in 1152px. Four steps pay for it, all of them solid step bars: 812@442 and 972@442 each lose their only inbound edge and 780@330 and 956@554 their only outbound — and 956@554, a flow origin with no inbound by design, is left with no edges at all — the only solid step bar in that condition, where the panel’s four other edgeless bars are dashed queue bars carrying none by design. So the flow does not read as continuous through any of them. QADrawing rules — checked before any panel here is called done
Nearly every fault worth fixing in this set came from one of twenty things, so they are written down rather than rediscovered. The numbers are in the drawing's own units at the type sizes the kit states; shrink the type and every number moves. Anyone adding a panel walks the list before calling it finished, and the block at the foot is the set of faults that mean redraw rather than nudge.
5aThe list
Quality assurance
Twenty rules, then the reject list
CLEARANCE
01Every connector ends 8px clear of the node it points at and starts 8px clear of the node it leaves. An arrowhead never touches a box. 02No connector crosses a node or a label. Where the direct line would, route through the nearest clear corridor and enter the target from its side. Crossing a phase boundary is fine — that is what a transition does. 03A connector never approaches a node on the side its label occupies. On a bar row that side is the top, so a handoff leaves and enters at the bottom or the side — never the top. Where a terminus lands beside a text run it stands 8px off that run’s painted box, halo included; an arrow that begins or ends inside a label is the failure this rule exists to catch. 04A node label clears its box by 8px, measured to the descender, not the baseline — and to the outside of the knockout halo where it has one, which is 2px past the descender. Measured, not assumed: a 10.5px label paints 3.5px below its baseline, so a haloed one paints 5.5px below it and wants a 14px gutter to hold 8px of air. 05A label that rides a connector sets its baseline 8px off the line — 4.4px of air under the descender at 10px, the same in every panel. Cardinality markers are the exception: they sit against the line they annotate. 06Measure all of the above against painted extent, not the bounding box: a knockout halo adds 2px on every side, a depth shadow adds 4px below and to the right.
PADDING
0716px from a node border to its first mark; 14px from its last text to the far border. A field-list node — a title, a rule, then rows — is a different species: 14px on the sides, its rule 32px below the top border, rows on a 24px pitch, and 7px of air under the last row’s descender. There the pitch sets the bottom inset, not the padding number. 08On a two-line node both lines share one left edge. A leading dot sits outside that edge, never on it. 0910px minimum from any text to the panel frame. A phase divider is a hairline, not a container: text sits 6px off it, and symmetrically — the phase name 6px right of its divider, the duration 6px left of the next. A node’s own border is rule 07’s business, not this one.
TYPE
1012px minimum for a node’s title line at 900 and above; 10px is the floor in the 600-wide compact panels. 9px is the metadata tier and nothing else — units, axis labels, dwell times, and key or state markers set inside a node. 11Hierarchy is size and weight. The first line is 600, the second is 400 — and both are white. 12Grey #7d7d7d belongs to axis ticks, legends and captions. Never to a node’s own content.
ALIGNMENT
13One left edge per panel: the drawing, its how-to-read line, its legend and its caption all start at the same x. 14Nodes in a row share a top edge; labels in a row share a baseline. 15Sibling nodes that mean the same kind of thing share a width.
COLOUR
16Teal is work running, yellow is waiting, red is stalled, grey dashed is nothing running. No other hue carries meaning. 17Colour encodes one variable. Size may encode one more, and the panel has to say which.
EVERY PANEL CARRIES
18A one-line how-to-read above the drawing. 19A legend at the foot naming every colour and mark that appears in it. 20A caption stating what the drawing argues, not what it contains.
After resizing any box, re-measure every mark that was placed against its old edges — connectors, cardinality markers and labels all carry those coordinates and none of them move on their own.
REDRAW, DO NOT NUDGE, IF
an arrowhead overlaps text a connector crosses a box labels in one row sit at different baselines a node’s own content is grey the legend omits a mark that appears equal boxes on a grid carry unequal quantities
Written from the faults actually found in turns 1 to 4, not from a style guide. Rules 01 to 05 account for most of them: an arrowhead resting on a label is the single most common defect, because the standoff was set against the box and the label was never measured. The reject list is separate because those six are not finishing errors — each one means the drawing is encoding something it has not earned, and adjusting the geometry hides it rather than fixing it.
4Density — how many nodes the kit actually holds
Every arrow now stands 8px clear of the node it leaves and the node it enters, which is what stops a dense canvas from reading as a solid mass. Past roughly sixteen nodes that is not enough on its own: equal boxes on a grid read as a table, so every drawing below gives its blocks a real quantity to be sized by. Below, the same lifecycle at 24 states and at 40, and a flow at 24 steps, drawn at full size so the ceiling is visible rather than asserted. Colour carries one meaning across all four: teal is moving, yellow is waiting, red is stalled, grey dashed is terminal, and every panel states it again at its own foot.
4aState machine at 24 · 1200 · time-proportional blocks, instants as points, S-linked
Lifecycle · 24 states
A marketplace order, drawn on its own clock
read left to right inside a phase band, then down to the next band · block width is time spent, on a log scale
colour is what is happening: teal work running, yellow waiting, red stalled, dashed nothing running
Equal boxes on a grid make a spreadsheet, so the boxes are not equal: each block is as wide as the time the order spends in it, on a log scale. Instant machine states collapse to slivers with the name turned on its side, waits stretch out, and the silhouette becomes the argument — one payment hold is wider than the whole of picking, packing and labelling together. Colour and form carry the rest: teal where work is actually running, yellow where the order is only waiting on someone, red where it has stalled, dashed edges where nothing is running at all, a double stroke on the terminal state, a ring on the four states reported outside the company, a red tick where an order can still cancel. Instant states are not thin boxes, they are points — a state with no duration has nothing to be wide about — so every label stays horizontal. No two neighbours share a baseline, which makes each connector a symmetric S leaving one right edge and arriving at the next left edge, curving only because it has to.
4bThe same machine at 40 · 900 · five stages, band thickness = accounts
Lifecycle · 40 states → 5 stages
Ten thousand signups, one year on
Forty states will not fit as forty boxes, so the machine is drawn as the volume moving through it, every band to scale against the same thousand-account rule. The trunk loses roughly half its thickness at each stage, which is the whole reading: 260px of signup arrives as 23px of retained account. The four losses peel to one terminal in trunk order so no band crosses another, and the win-back is a real band carrying its own width back into Trial.
4cFlow at 24 steps · 1200 · five owner lanes on one continuous day axis
Process · 24 steps · 5 owners
Enterprise onboarding, first sixty days
One axis, no wrapping: every step sits at the day it happens, so position alone answers when. Lanes are owners, which is what a column is supposed to mean, and each arc is a handoff — sixteen of them, standing 8px clear of the bar it leaves and the bar it enters. The long jumps into the customer lane are followed by the yellow bars, which is the finding. The strip at the foot is the same sixty days projected onto one line.
