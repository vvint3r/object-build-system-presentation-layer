Icons

One glyph family, one fill rule, and a short list of the places a glyph may appear. The family is a 24px outline set that ships a solid twin for every shape, which is what makes the accent fill possible without redrawing a single glyph.
SourceBasil Icons community set · 497 glyphs · 24px grid · every shape ships a solid twin
ss: 01

The set and its treatment

A glyph is black, drawn on a 24px grid at 1.5px. The accent enters as a fill above a waterline at 13 of 24 — one number for the whole family, never set per glyph. The solid twin supplies the fill and the outline is drawn over it, so a filled glyph and a plain one are the same shape at the same weight.

The two states

Every glyph is drawn once and appears in both states. The fill is the only difference between the rows.

How the fill is drawn

Three operations, in this order, on the pair of files the family already ships.
01The solid twin is clipped to the top 13 units of the 24-unit box and filled in the accent.
02The outline is drawn over it at full height, unchanged, so the silhouette and every interior detail stay black.
03Nothing else moves. The waterline is a constant of the family, not a property of a glyph or a placement.

Where the fill starts

At 16px the band is under 9px tall and reads as a printing error rather than a mark, so the fill is dropped and the glyph runs as outline only.

ss: 02

The ration

The accent is reserved by rule, so a family that carried it everywhere would spend the reservation on decoration. Four limits keep the fill a mark rather than a texture.
The fill appears at 20px and above. Below that the glyph is outline only.
One filled glyph per view, at the same altitude as the reserved offset shadow.
Only the placements in ss: 03 may carry it, and a grid of peers never does.
The fill is never the only cue. The glyph's shape and its mono label carry the meaning without it.

What the fill must survive

Three conditions the treatment was set against, and what it costs in each.
The smallest working size. At 24px the band is 13px tall; at 16px it is under 9px, which is why 20px is the floor for a filled glyph.
Greyscale. On paper the band prints as one tone and nothing is lost, because a filled glyph and a plain one are the same shape and the label states the kind.
Cost per glyph. Zero. The solid twin already knocks its own interior detail out, so no glyph is redrawn and a new one joins the family with no extra work.
ss: 03

Placement

A glyph belongs in four places. Everywhere else the label carries the meaning on its own, and a glyph beside it either repeats the label or competes with it. Where two of these placements meet in one view, the fill goes to the one higher in the register.

Where a glyph is allowed

Four placements, each with the size it runs at and whether the fill is available there. The glyphs themselves are in ss: 04.
PlacementSizeFillWhat the glyph adds
Callout label 20 px Allowed The kind of aside is visible before the word is read, which is what makes a callout skippable.
Empty state 32 px Allowed A surface with nothing in it still carries a mark, so the absence reads as deliberate rather than broken.
Index or hub card 24 px Outline only One destination separates from its neighbours. These are peers in a grid, so a fill per card is the texture the ration forbids.
Register row label 20 px Outline only The family a row belongs to, where that family repeats down a column and the words would repeat with it.

Never carries a glyph

Six placements where a glyph was tried in earlier drafts and removed.
Inside a sentence. A glyph in running text breaks the line box and is read as punctuation.
A nav pill or a tab. The pill is already the shape that marks it; a glyph inside competes with its own label.
A table cell. Cells carry mono values, and a glyph in one column makes that column a different kind of data.
A status chip. The tint and the mono word are the two cues the chip is built from; a third is noise.
A button. The label states the action; a glyph beside it either repeats the label or contradicts it.
A list bullet. Bullets are a teal dot or a black square by rule, and a glyph in that position is read as a bullet anyway.
ss: 04

The register

Every glyph the system uses, and the one concept it stands for. This is a closed list on the same terms as the label vocabulary: a concept that is not in it takes no glyph, and no two concepts share a shape. A placement being available does not entitle the material in it to a glyph.

How a concept earns a glyph

Four conditions, all of which must hold before a shape is added.
It is already a closed-vocabulary name. A glyph marks a callout label, a status, a template or an empty condition — never a subject, a topic or a technology.
It repeats. A concept that appears once does not need a mark; the word is shorter to read than a shape is to learn.
The shape is unclaimed. Two concepts on one shape means neither can be recognised, so the family reuses nothing and the register is checked before a glyph is drawn.
Its solid twin floods an area above the waterline. On a glyph drawn as a stroke — a check, a magnifier, a bolt — the solid twin is nearly the outline, so the black outline covers the band and the fill is invisible. Those shapes stay out of the fill placements.
