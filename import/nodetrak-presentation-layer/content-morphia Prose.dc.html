The pieces a passage needs that are still prose: a sentence borrowed from a source, a sentence of our own set larger, the two marks that interrupt a line, and the three lists whose shape carries an argument. Each one is a decision about where the words came from, and each has exactly one form.
Components 7 primitives · 6 rules · specimens from the freshness practice
Two things look alike on the page and mean opposite things. A quotation carries words this document did not write, so it is bounded and attributed. A pull quote carries a sentence this document already wrote, set larger so a scanning reader meets it, so it is unbounded and never attributed. Confusing the two makes the document appear to cite itself.

Bracket and mono attribution, no fill and no outline. The bracket is the same sub-block marker used everywhere else, so a quotation reads as a bounded piece of someone else's text rather than a decorated aside.

Teal bar and Nunito 800 at 21px, capped at 24 characters a line. No quotation marks, because nothing is being quoted, and no attribution, because the author is this document. A pull quote that says something the passage does not already say is new content in a decorative slot.
Two superscripts interrupt a sentence and they are not interchangeable. A citation marker points outward at the evidence behind the claim it follows. A footnote points downward at an aside the sentence could not hold. A reader who cannot tell which is which has to open both to find out.

Citation marker

A count, never a sequence number, set in mono with a teal underline and opening a source panel in place. It attaches to the claim, which means it sits after the number or the assertion, not at the end of the paragraph.

Footnote

A bracketed ordinal with no underline, resolving in a notes block at the end of its own section rather than the end of the document. A section carries at most three; a fourth means the aside belongs in the passage.
A plain bulleted list makes no claim beyond membership. Three shapes claim more than that, and each earns its extra structure only when the claim is true: that the order matters, that the items come in opposed pairs, or that one item contains another.

Nested · one level, and only for containment

A child indents only when it is part of its parent rather than the next thing after it. Depth stops at two: a third level is a table, or the parent is a heading and the list starts again beneath it.
